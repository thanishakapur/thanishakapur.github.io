Fenwick & West LLP/Jul 2026 - Present

Which newly filed complaints does an attorney actually need to see?

Every attorney had a different answer, and not one of them was written down.

The problem

Attorneys were tracking newly filed complaints by checking legal data sources by hand and forwarding anything that looked relevant. The process was slow, it varied from person to person, and none of it existed in a form a system could use.

What I did

I interviewed attorneys and operations staff to get their unwritten criteria stated explicitly, then turned those criteria into filter logic and a scheduled pipeline. It pulls from legal data sources, matches new filings against the criteria, and sends every match to the relevant attorney as an Outlook alert.

What made it hard

The criteria people describe in a meeting are not the same criteria that hold up against real filings. Most of the iteration went into cutting false positives without dropping the filings that actually mattered. If attorneys stop trusting the alerts, they start ignoring them, and then the workflow is worse than the manual routine it replaced.

Teachers Supporting Teachers/Jun 2026 - Aug 2026

What does retention mean when four groups define it differently?

Each group had built its own definition, so the same word meant something different in every report.

The problem

Retention, leadership, and staff-capacity outcomes were tracked across four stakeholder groups, each with its own working definition. Those numbers were going into funder reporting, grant materials, and the SY25-26 annual report, so inconsistent definitions created a real credibility problem with funders.

What I did

I audited program data across all four groups, reconciled the competing definitions into one set everyone could report against, and built the standardized measures into Tableau dashboards and the annual report. Separately, I rebuilt the Salesforce CRM around the school recruitment pipeline, so prospective school partners, key decision-makers, and outreach history all lived in one place.

What made it hard

Standardizing a metric means somebody's existing number has to change. Getting agreement on which definition to adopt took as much work as implementing it did.

Chicago Park District, via campusCATALYST/Mar 2026 - Jun 2026

Which fieldhouses are understaffed relative to the rest of the system?

Without a shared scale, no one could compare any one site against the other 231.

The problem

Staffed-hour planning across 232 fieldhouses was being decided one site at a time. Without a common scale, there was no way to ask whether a given site was under-resourced compared to the rest of the system, and that comparison is exactly what resource allocation depends on.

What I did

I built an Excel decision-support tool that consolidates and scores seven operational metrics into a single comparable measure per site, and ran stakeholder interviews to surface the implementation constraints a purely quantitative model would have missed. The deliverable was the scoring tool plus recommendations the district could act on.

What made it hard

A composite score is only useful if it does not hide the tradeoff underneath it. The tool keeps every component metric visible next to the total, so a planner can see whether a site scores low on one dimension or on all seven. Those two situations call for completely different responses.

Scale232 fieldhouses, 7 operational metrics

UChicago Memory Research Lab/Nov 2025 - Present

Which survey responses are clean enough to analyze?

That depends entirely on the validation rules, which is why the rules themselves have to be written down and applied identically every time.

The problem

Raw Prolific and JATOS exports are not analysis-ready. Before anyone can make a statistical claim, someone has to decide which responses count, flag the ones that do not, and reshape the exports into participant-level and item-level datasets. That has to happen every time new data comes in.

What I did

I built Python ETL and validation pipelines that turn raw exports into analysis-ready datasets, automated the quality checks across a 650-item cognitive assessment, and generated the figures showing performance patterns and experimental effects for a coauthored paper in development.

What made it hard

Validation rules encode judgment calls about what counts as a real response. Writing those rules down and applying them consistently is what makes a finding reproducible, rather than dependent on whoever happened to clean the data that week.

How I work

All four projects follow the same pattern. An organization makes the same decision over and over, informally. It is expensive because it is manual, and inconsistent because everyone does it slightly differently. My job is to sit with the people making that decision, get the rule written down in a form that holds up against real data, build the thing that runs it, and then stick around long enough to find out whether anyone uses it.

That last part matters more than it sounds. Getting a pipeline to run is easier than getting people to use it, and only the second one changes how an organization works.

Long term I want to work in technology IP and patent law. Right now I am building the part of that work most lawyers never get, which is a working understanding of how these systems are actually put together.
